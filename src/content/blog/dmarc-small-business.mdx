Research consistently finds the same result: the majority of small business domains have no DMARC protection. Estimates from email security organizations place the figure at 70% or higher for domains belonging to businesses with fewer than 100 employees.

That number means that most small businesses — most dental practices, law firms, accounting offices, restaurants, and retail shops — can be impersonated by anyone with a laptop and basic technical knowledge. Their domain name can appear in the From field of a phishing email sent to thousands of their clients, and nothing in their DNS configuration will stop it.

This is not a theoretical risk. It is an active attack vector used daily.

## TL;DR

- Research consistently finds that over 70% of small business domains have no DMARC protection, leaving them open to email spoofing by anyone who tries.
- Without DMARC, attackers can send email that displays your business name and domain in the From field — to your clients, patients, or customers — and it will be delivered.
- The most common attacks enabled by missing DMARC include phishing campaigns targeting your clients and business email compromise (BEC) fraud targeting wire transfers.
- Healthcare, legal, financial services, and real estate businesses are disproportionately targeted because of the high value of the transactions and data involved.
- Business email compromise attacks cost small businesses an average of $125,000 to $200,000 per incident, and these losses are typically not covered by standard business insurance.
- DMARC is free to implement and takes about 20 minutes to set up at the initial monitoring level.
- The full path to protection — SPF, DKIM, and DMARC at p=reject — can be completed by most small businesses within 4-8 weeks.
- A professional network security audit includes a check of your DMARC, SPF, and DKIM status as part of a full email security assessment.

## Why are most small business domains unprotected?

The most common reason is that no one flagged it as a problem.

DMARC is a DNS configuration — it sits in the background of your internet infrastructure, completely invisible in day-to-day operations. Legitimate email flows normally whether or not DMARC exists. There is no error message, no dashboard alert, no warning that your domain is unprotected. The only time the absence becomes visible is when something goes wrong.

Most IT support providers focus on keeping systems operational — making sure email is delivered, devices are connected, and applications are running. Proactive security auditing, including email authentication review, falls outside the typical managed service agreement unless the client specifically requests it.

Domain registrars and hosting providers do not automatically configure DMARC. When you set up a domain and start sending email, you are responsible for publishing SPF, DKIM, and DMARC records yourself or directing your IT provider to do it. Most business owners do not know to ask.

The result is a large population of small business domains that have been sending email for years — sometimes decades — with no email authentication whatsoever.

## What can attackers actually do with an unprotected domain?

The attacks are well-documented and continue to increase in volume.

**Phishing campaigns targeting your clients.** An attacker who knows your clients' email addresses (often findable from a business website, public records, or prior data breaches) sends a message from your domain. The email might tell dental patients their insurance information needs to be updated, ask law firm clients to log in to review a document, or tell restaurant loyalty members their account has a new reward. Because the From address shows your practice name and domain, many recipients comply.

**Business email compromise fraud.** BEC is one of the most financially devastating cybercrimes targeting small businesses. The attack typically works as follows: an attacker spoofs an email from a business owner or manager to an accountant, vendor, or employee responsible for financial transactions. The email requests a wire transfer, changes vendor payment details, or redirects payroll. Because the From address appears authentic, the recipient processes the request. Losses are often unrecoverable.

The FBI's Internet Crime Complaint Center reports that BEC costs businesses billions of dollars annually, with average losses per incident well above $100,000.

**Damaging your deliverability.** When spam filter networks detect spoofed email being sent in your name, they may flag your domain as a source of suspicious mail — even though the spoofed messages were not sent by you. Your legitimate outbound email then begins landing in spam folders, hurting your business communications.

**Regulatory exposure.** In healthcare, a phishing email sent from a spoofed medical practice address that results in a patient providing credentials or clicking a malicious link may trigger a HIPAA breach investigation — even though your practice sent no such email. The spoofing incident involves patient communications from your domain, and regulators may look to the practice to explain what controls were in place.

## Which industries are most at risk?

Not all small businesses face the same risk profile. Attackers target domains where the payoff is highest and client trust is strongest.

### Medical and dental offices

Healthcare is among the most targeted sectors for email-based attacks. Patients extend implicit trust to communications from their providers — they expect and act on appointment reminders, billing notices, and insurance requests. An email appearing to come from a dental practice asking a patient to confirm their insurance details is entirely plausible.

Healthcare businesses are also subject to HIPAA, which requires protecting the confidentiality of patient health information. A spoofing attack that results in patient data being exposed — even through no fault of the practice — can trigger investigation and penalties.

### Law firms

Law firms hold highly sensitive client information and regularly communicate about confidential matters. Email impersonation targeting clients of a law firm can be used to intercept settlement communications, redirect funds, or obtain sensitive case information from clients who believe they are communicating with their attorney.

State bar associations increasingly treat email security as part of an attorney's duty of competence in managing client data.

### Accounting and CPA firms

Tax season creates a reliable window for email impersonation targeting accounting clients. Clients expect urgent email from their accountants around filing deadlines, and attackers exploit this by sending spoofed emails requesting that clients provide sensitive financial data, confirm wire transfer details, or log in to a malicious portal.

### Real estate offices

Real estate businesses are the single most targeted industry for wire fraud via email. A buyer in escrow expects email communications about wire transfer instructions. A spoofed email arriving at precisely the right moment in the transaction cycle — appearing to be from the title company, the agent, or the attorney — can redirect hundreds of thousands of dollars to an attacker's account. These funds are almost never recovered.

### Retail businesses with POS systems

Retail businesses that process card payments hold PCI-DSS obligations and are targets for attacks aimed at gaining access to point-of-sale systems or customer payment data. Email impersonation is a common initial vector for these attacks.

## What does the attack actually look like?

A small accounting firm has a domain: `bestcpaservice.com`. They have SPF set up but no DMARC.

An attacker identifies the firm's clients from LinkedIn, public filing records, and the firm's website. The attacker sets up a mail server — perfectly legal to do — and crafts an email from `billing@bestcpaservice.com`. Because there is no DMARC record, major mail providers have no policy guidance from the domain owner about what to do with unverified mail. Many deliver it.

The email tells clients their tax filing package is ready, but their account needs to be verified before it can be downloaded. A link is provided. Clients click it, enter their login credentials, and their financial portal access is compromised.

The firm finds out weeks later when a client calls asking why their bank account was accessed. By then, the damage is done.

This scenario is not hypothetical — it plays out constantly, against businesses that are running normally and have no idea their domain is being used against their clients.

## How does DMARC stop this?

DMARC stops this attack by adding an enforcement layer to email authentication. When a DMARC record with `p=reject` is published, receiving mail servers check whether incoming mail claiming to be from `bestcpaservice.com` can be authenticated using SPF or DKIM — with the additional requirement that the authenticated domain aligns with the visible From address.

If authentication fails or alignment is violated, the mail server applies the DMARC policy: reject the message. It never reaches the inbox.

For the attacker, this means there is no longer a viable attack path using that domain. They cannot send spoofed mail that will be delivered. The attack fails before it reaches the client.

## What should a small business do right now?

The immediate action is to find out where you stand.

**Step 1:** Go to mxtoolbox.com/DMARC.aspx and enter your domain. If the result is "No DMARC Record Found," your domain is unprotected.

**Step 2:** Check your SPF record by running `dig TXT yourdomain.com` or using MXToolbox's SPF lookup. Look for a record starting with `v=spf1`.

**Step 3:** If either is missing or if you are not sure how to interpret the results, contact your IT provider or DNS administrator and ask them to audit your email authentication configuration.

**Step 4:** Implement DMARC at `p=none` with a reporting address to begin monitoring your email traffic. Review reports for 2-4 weeks, then move toward `p=reject`.

A comprehensive network security audit will check your DMARC, SPF, and DKIM status as part of the full assessment — along with every other aspect of your network's security posture. For businesses that handle sensitive client data, this is the fastest way to identify all your email authentication gaps and get a clear picture of where you stand.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Why are small businesses more vulnerable to email spoofing than large companies?

Large enterprises typically have dedicated security teams that audit email authentication as a matter of routine. Small businesses rarely have this resource. Email authentication records like DMARC, SPF, and DKIM are DNS configurations that fall outside the day-to-day work of most IT support providers — they keep systems running, but may not proactively audit security configurations unless asked. The result is that small business domains frequently go unprotected for years without anyone noticing.

### What industries are most targeted by email spoofing attacks?

Healthcare, legal, financial, and real estate businesses are disproportionately targeted. Medical and dental offices hold HIPAA-regulated patient data and clients trust urgent communications from their providers. Law firms handle privileged communications and settlement funds. Accounting firms are targeted around tax filing season. Real estate offices process wire transfers, making them prime targets for business email compromise fraud — where a spoofed email redirects a buyer's wire to a criminal account.

### How much does email spoofing cost small businesses?

Business email compromise (BEC) — which often relies on email spoofing — costs small businesses an average of $125,000 to $200,000 per incident according to FBI Internet Crime Complaint Center data. These losses are typically not covered by standard business insurance. Beyond direct financial loss, spoofing attacks that use your domain to target clients can result in reputational damage, client churn, and in regulated industries, regulatory investigations.

### Is DMARC difficult to set up for a small business?

No. The initial implementation — adding a DMARC record at `p=none` to begin monitoring — takes about 20 minutes for someone with access to your DNS settings. The more involved part is the monitoring phase: reviewing aggregate reports over 2-4 weeks to identify all services sending email on your behalf before moving to enforcement. Most small businesses can complete the full implementation to `p=reject` within 4-8 weeks. A network security assessment will identify your current DMARC status and any gaps in your SPF and DKIM configuration.

### Can attackers spoof my email even if I have SPF set up?

Yes. SPF without DMARC does not prevent spoofing of the visible From address that your clients see. SPF verifies the technical envelope sender (the return-path address used for bounces), not the human-readable From field. Attackers can send mail from a server that passes SPF for their own domain while displaying your business name and email address in the From field. DMARC's alignment requirement is what prevents this, by requiring that the authenticated domain match the visible From address.
